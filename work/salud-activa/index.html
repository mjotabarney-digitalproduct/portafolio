Ask once, not twenty times

The next plan to move onto the new in-house sales flow came with a long health questionnaire. With up to 20 people on one policy, the real design problem was multiplication.

Role
Product Designer (UX/UI), Indra Group
Timeline
Jan to Jul 2026
Team
Client UX lead, PM, functional analyst, engineering, business
Tools
Figma, FigJam, Excel; client design library
Status
Delivered to development; not in production at handoff

Problem

Each person on a policy (up to 20) had to answer the full health questionnaire separately. The business had redefined the questions; the flow had to collect all of it and get drastically shorter.

What I did

Inverted the question model, branched the questionnaire, redesigned how answers are verified, accepted and edited, and proposed the design of the transactional emails for code verification and payment recovery.

Outcome

My model became the final one, chosen over an infinite-scroll, one-questionnaire-per-person approach that was easier to build. One design serves two sibling plans.

01Context

Same flow, much heavier form

After digital sales moved in-house, this plan also had to adopt the new flow. Unlike the first two, it required a health declaration: 31 conditional questions spanning general health, habits and specialties such as ophthalmology, ENT, neurology, cardiovascular and respiratory.

A policy could include up to 20 people, and before, every one of them answered the entire questionnaire on their own. The cost grows with every person you add, and those are exactly the people the business most wants to enroll.

02The idea

Flip the question

Before · per person

“Has Jane been hospitalized in the last 3 months?” “Has Joe?” “Has Joe Senior?” Repeated for every question, for every person.

After · per question

“Has the policyholder or any beneficiary been hospitalized in the last 3 months?” Yes → “Select who.” Everyone not selected is a No. Details are asked only for the yeses.

The common case, healthy people, gets the shortest path: one answer covers the whole family. The rare case gets exactly the follow-up questions it needs, attached to the right person. A No at a root question closes its branch entirely.

I also redesigned how the questionnaire is verified, accepted and edited: a summary grouped in accordions by specialty, with inline editing, so reviewing twenty people’s answers doesn’t mean starting over.

03The hard call

Easier to build versus easier to finish

The option on the table was an infinite scroll with one questionnaire per person, simpler to develop. I didn’t argue it was ugly. I argued with two kinds of evidence:

  1. A 35-page benchmark of how other companies handle long forms, especially on mobile: progress indicators compared across N26, Oscar and Stripe Atlas, and explicit criteria for multi-step versus single page: more than 15 fields or complex data call for steps; fewer than 10 with expert users can live on one page.
  2. The arithmetic of how many questions users would no longer have to answer by hand under the inverted model.

The inverted model became the final model.

04Beyond the screen

Part of the flow happens in the inbox

Some steps don’t happen in the product: the verification code, a rejected payment, the reminder to come back and finish. The business supplied the content of the transactional emails; I proposed the design, in desktop and responsive variants, organized by process state. The 48-hour window to resume was set by the business after checking technical feasibility.

05Outcome

One design, two plans

A sibling plan didn’t get its own prototypes for lack of time, but the two are similar enough that one development serves both, so the impact of the model extends to both products.
