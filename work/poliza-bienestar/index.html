Two vendors, one product, and the gap nobody owned

A one-year, fully digital preventive-health policy for a multinational health company. I designed the landing, the purchase form and most of the app, and documented the problems that weren’t mine to fix but would land on the user anyway.

Role
Product Designer (UX/UI), Indra Group
Timeline
Jul to Oct 2026
Team
Client UX lead, PMO, functional analyst, two platform vendors
Tools
Figma, FigJam, Excel; client design library
Status
Handed off with UX at 85%; not yet in production

Problem

A brand-new product with a December launch expectation, where purchase lives with one vendor and everyday use with another, and nobody owns the moment in between.

What I did

A strategic market benchmark, the landing, the purchase form and most of the app (diagnostic, health score, benefits, appointments with quota balances), plus a written record of the vendor split and six unfiled scope changes.

Outcome

At handoff the UX front was at 85% while the project sat at 55% against an expected 78%. Delivered to my successor with every pending flow named.

01Context

A product, not a checkout

My first two projects for this client were sales flows. This one was different: an individual, one-year policy built around preventing illness rather than treating it. Three plans; a medical component with a family doctor and lab tests capped by per-plan quotas; unlimited one-on-one virtual coaching across life, emotional, physical, financial and tax topics; a psychosocial helpline; a community; and a digital platform with content, a health index, challenges and rewards.

It was a top priority for the client’s leadership, with a launch expected in December. That combination of a new product, high visibility and a fixed date is exactly where undefined decisions get quietly pushed onto design.

02Research

A benchmark of markets, not of screens

Before drawing anything I compared the wellness offers of national insurers with international Vitality-style programs. The useful output wasn’t a moodboard; it was three framing findings:

  1. There are two different product categories hiding under the word “wellness”, and the client’s offer had to be clear about which one it was.
  2. Colombian regulation treats a health insurance policy and prepaid medicine as different things, supervised by different authorities, and each implies different UX requirements.
  3. A fully digital wellness policy with coaching and gamification was white space in Colombia. No local reference to copy, which raised the bar on getting the basics right.
03The seam

Buying and using lived in two different places

From July I flagged that the experience had two separate moments, buying the policy and living with it, and that the separation was not conceptual but physical. One vendor ran the purchase in a closed portal where customization stopped at colors and logos. Another ran everyday use in a responsive web app with no store listing, no native push notifications and no organic discovery.

The user would pay in one product and wake up in another. Nobody’s backlog contained the walk between them.

I couldn’t fix a vendor architecture from a design seat. What I could do was make the gap impossible to miss: I documented it, with its design implications, in its own section of the handoff so it would be treated as a product decision rather than discovered in production.

04Design

Turning business rules into something a person can read

The health score, the Wheel of Life, the quota logic and the complaints flow came defined in user stories. My job was to turn each rule into an interface a non-expert could trust:

  • A wellbeing diagnostic across five dimensions (nutrition, sleep, activity, physical and mental health) that produces a score out of 1,000 and a Wheel of Life. Because it’s long, the user can pause and resume where they left off.
  • Appointments that spend quotas. Each booking shows the remaining balance, and cancelling returns the quota. That rule has to be visible, or it feels like money disappearing.
  • A complaints and requests flow with a filing number and status tracking. Here I went beyond the stories and proposed the help content to the business, so users could solve more on their own.
  • Accessibility as default: contrast and text-size controls, and a pause control on the auto-advancing services carousel.
  • A screen-naming system used consistently across the file so developers and my successor could reference any screen without ambiguity.
05The hard call

Six scope changes nobody filed

Over three months, six changes reshaped what I was designing, and none of them went through a formal change request: the app’s format; a customer self-service capability the business rejected; the sale moving to the purchase vendor, which turned my landing into an informational page; authentication left undefined; electronic signature; and plan parametrization.

Why it mattered

In a project with its own PMO, an untracked change doesn’t disappear. It becomes rework that looks like a designer running late. Writing the six changes down, with their impact on design, moved them from “design debt” to “decisions someone has to make.”

I also structured and documented the UX front’s own schedule, so progress could be reported against something concrete rather than a feeling.

06Outcome

Where it stood when I handed it over

85%UX front complete at handoff
55%Overall project progress
78%Progress the project expected by then

Complete: landing for desktop and responsive, the purchase form with a navigable prototype, and most of the app: components, onboarding, diagnostic quiz, policy detail, home, profile and benefits. Pending, and handed to my successor by name on October 20, 2026: booking and cancelling appointments, call requests, content, complaints and support, the login flow and prototype wiring. The score’s formula was still awaiting definition by product.

What I can’t claimThe product hadn’t launched when my contract ended, so there are no usage or business metrics. What I can show is the state of the work and the decisions it made visible.
07Next

If I had another month

I’d prototype and test the walk between the two vendors, from “payment approved” to the first useful moment in the app, because that’s where a preventive product either earns a habit or gets forgotten.
