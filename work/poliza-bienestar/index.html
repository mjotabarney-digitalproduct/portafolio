Two vendors, one product, and the gap nobody owned

A one-year, fully digital preventive-health policy. I designed the landing, the purchase form and most of the app, and put in writing the problems that weren’t mine to fix but would land on the user.

Role
Product Designer (UX/UI), Indra Group
Timeline
Jul to Oct 2026
Team
Client UX, PMO, functional analyst and two vendors
Tools
Figma, FigJam, Excel
Status
Handed off with UX at 85%, not in production

Problem

A new product due in December: purchase lives with one vendor, everyday use with another, and nobody owns the step between them.

What I did

A market benchmark, the landing, the purchase form and most of the app, plus a written record of the vendor split and six unfiled scope changes.

Outcome

UX at 85% at handoff, with the project at 55% against an expected 78%, and every pending flow named for my successor.

01Context

A product, not a checkout

My first two projects for this client were sales flows. This was a whole product built to prevent illness: three plans, a family doctor and lab tests with quotas, unlimited virtual coaching, a psychosocial helpline, a community and a platform with content, a health index, challenges and rewards.

It was a priority for the client’s leadership, with a December date. New product, high visibility, fixed date: exactly where undefined decisions quietly land on design.

02Research

A benchmark of markets, not of screens

I compared national insurers’ wellness offers with international Vitality-style programs. Three findings came out of it:

  1. “Wellness” hides two different product categories, and the offer had to be clear about which one it was.
  2. In Colombia, a health policy and prepaid medicine have different regulators, and each asks different things of the experience.
  3. A fully digital wellness policy with coaching and gamification didn’t exist in the country. With no local reference, the basics had to be very well solved.
03The seam

Buying and using lived in two different places

From July I flagged that buying the policy and using it were two separate moments, and not just in theory. One vendor ran purchase in a closed portal that only allowed colors and logos. Another ran everyday use in a web app with no store listing, no push notifications and no organic discovery.

The user would pay in one product and wake up in another. Nobody’s backlog contained the walk between them.

I couldn’t change the vendor architecture from design, but I could make the gap impossible to miss: I documented it with its implications in its own section of the handoff, so it would be treated as a product decision instead of discovered in production.

04Design

Business rules a person can read

The score, the Wheel of Life, quotas and complaints came defined in user stories. My job was to turn each rule into an interface people could trust:

  • A wellbeing diagnostic across five dimensions that gives a score out of 1,000 and a Wheel of Life. It’s long, so it can be paused and resumed.
  • Appointments with quotas. Each booking shows the balance, and cancelling returns the quota. If it isn’t visible, it feels like money disappearing.
  • Complaints and requests with a filing number and tracking. Here I also proposed the help content, so users could solve more on their own.
  • Accessibility by default: contrast, text size and a pause control on the auto-advancing carousel.
  • Consistent screen naming, so developers and my successor always meant the same screen.
05The hard call

Six scope changes nobody filed

In three months, six changes reshaped my work without a formal change request: the app’s format, a self-service capability the business rejected, the sale moving to the vendor and turning my landing informational, undefined authentication, electronic signature and plan parametrization.

Why it mattered

With its own PMO, an untracked change doesn’t disappear: it becomes rework that looks like design running late. Writing them down with their impact turned them into decisions someone had to make.

I also documented the UX front’s schedule, so progress was reported against something concrete.

06Outcome

Where it stood when I handed it over

85%UX front complete at handoff
55%Overall project progress
78%Progress the project expected by then

Done: the landing for desktop and responsive, the purchase form with a prototype and most of the app. Pending, and handed to my successor on October 20: appointments, call requests, content, complaints, login and prototype wiring. The score’s formula was still awaiting definition by product.

What I can’t claimThe product hadn’t launched when I left, so there are no usage metrics. I show the state of the work and the decisions it made visible.
07Next

If I had another month

I’d test the walk between the two vendors, from “payment approved” to the first useful moment in the app. That’s where a preventive product earns a habit or gets forgotten.
